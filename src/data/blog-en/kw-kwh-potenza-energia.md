An appliance rated at 800 watts tells you how quickly it can draw energy under the stated conditions. To understand the energy it uses for a task, you also need to know how long it draws that power.

This distinction matters for computers, appliances and batteries. It also comes up in [electricity demand flexibility](/blog/rete-elettrica-flessibilita-domanda) and when looking at how the [carbon intensity of electricity](/blog/elettricita-intensita-carbonica) changes over time.

## Work out the rate and the amount

Watts measure power, the rate at which energy is transferred. A kilowatt is 1,000 watts. A kilowatt-hour measures energy. It is the amount transferred by a steady power of one kilowatt over one hour.

Suppose a load constantly draws 800 W for three hours. Convert the power to 0.8 kW and multiply by the duration. The result is **0.8 kW × 3 h = 2.4 kWh**. That calculation assumes the power stays constant throughout.

## Account for a device that changes how much it draws

An appliance may switch between active periods and pauses or change its power according to its workload. The rated value is a specification, and a reading from one moment describes that moment. For variable demand, accumulate the power over the time interval. A suitable energy meter can give you that total directly.

## Compare the same activity

When comparing two computers doing the same job, include how long each one takes and the conditions of the measurement. A shorter task can use less energy even if the power is higher while it runs. For a battery, the capacity in Wh describes how much stored energy can be delivered under the specified conditions. Its power rating describes how quickly it can provide energy. Check the units, the duration and the work performed before comparing the figures.
