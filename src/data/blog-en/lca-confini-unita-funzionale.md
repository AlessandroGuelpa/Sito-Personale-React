Two products can come with environmental figures that look comparable. One may cover manufacturing, transport and disposal, while the other covers only use. Before comparing the numbers, check which service each study measures and which processes enter the calculation.

[Measuring food waste](/blog/spreco-alimentare-misurare-prima) offers another example of why the starting boundary matters. In a life cycle assessment, the [carbon intensity of the electricity](/blog/elettricita-intensita-carbonica) used by a process can change the comparison as well.

## Define the function the products share

A disposable container and a reusable one may both deliver a meal at a defined level of performance. The functional unit could represent a set number of those deliveries. Under that definition, one option may use many containers and the other may use a smaller number of containers repeatedly. Washing, transport, breakage and the actual number of returns become inputs. The material alone cannot determine the break-even point.

## Draw the system boundary

A boundary specifies which processes count and how far the study follows each one. It may include resource extraction, production, distribution, use and end-of-life treatment to the level of detail required by the study. The European Commission's Joint Research Centre develops environmental footprint methods that consider these stages together. Looking across stages can reveal when a change reduces work in one place and shifts it somewhere else.

## Check how sensitive the result is

Climate change is one impact category. Resource and water use can raise different questions. Report the categories that matter to the comparison and identify any conclusion that depends on an uncertain assumption. In the container example, I would vary the number of successful reuses and show where the result changes. A reader can then decide whether the study's assumed washing, transport and return conditions fit their situation.
