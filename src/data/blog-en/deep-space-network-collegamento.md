A photograph of a spacecraft shows its instruments, solar panels and antennas. Another part of that mission stays on Earth. Ground antennas receive data, mission teams decide when each vehicle can make contact, and operators manage the flow. The Deep Space Network gives NASA a way to work with spacecraft at great distances.

The [signal delay on a lunar link](/blog/luna-latenza-comunicazioni) affects when information can arrive. [Lunar launch windows](/blog/finestre-lancio-luna-orbite) add a different timing constraint that mission planners also need to account for.

## Build a link across a moving Earth

The network uses large antenna complexes at different locations. Their distribution helps cover spacecraft as Earth rotates. Each link depends on the geometry, the available signal power and the antennas at both ends. Planners allocate the ground equipment across missions, activities and contact opportunities.

## Receive science data and measure motion

A link can carry instrument readings or images. Radio observations also help track the spacecraft. The navigation team combines these measurements with other observations and models to refine its estimate of position and motion. Each measurement has uncertainty and only constrains part of what the team wants to know.

## Match the data to the available contact time

Suppose an instrument creates more data than a link can transmit in one session. The mission will need onboard storage, priorities for what to send first and a plan for the next contact. Improving the instrument's output in this example increases the amount of data waiting to be returned. A separate change would be needed to send more of it within the same window.

When I review a system, I follow the lines to the services it relies on. A spacecraft can collect excellent observations and still depend on a shared ground link with limited capacity. Knowing when the link is available and what it can carry shapes both the instrument plan and the operations plan.
