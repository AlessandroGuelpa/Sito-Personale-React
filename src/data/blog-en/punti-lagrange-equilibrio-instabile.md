A diagram can show a telescope sitting on a labelled point called L2. To understand the mission, start by asking which pair of bodies defines that point and how the spacecraft moves in relation to it. [Spacecraft position and attitude](/blog/navigazione-spaziale-posizione-orientamento) also depend on a reference frame. [Lunar launch windows](/blog/finestre-lancio-luna-orbite) illustrate a different orbital constraint, involving when a transfer can meet its destination.

## Use the frame that rotates with the two large bodies

In the circular restricted three-body model, consider two orbiting masses and a small third body whose effect on their motion can be ignored. Lagrange points are equilibria in the reference frame rotating with the larger bodies. The Sun–Earth system has its own five points. The Earth–Moon system has another set. The label L2 only identifies the place after you know which system is meant.

## Distinguish equilibrium from stability

In this model, small disturbances around L1, L2 or L3 can grow. Spacecraft operating nearby can follow orbits around the region and use station-keeping manoeuvres to stay on an acceptable path. L4 and L5 have different stability properties that depend, among other things, on the mass ratio of the two main bodies.

## Look at how Webb uses the geometry

Webb orbits in the vicinity of Sun–Earth L2. That region helps keep the Sun and Earth on the same side of its sunshield while the telescope observes. Station-keeping and operating constraints still belong in the mission design. The geometry is valuable because it makes some goals easier to meet and leaves other jobs for the spacecraft and its operators.

When I read an orbital diagram, I use the point to locate the region. To understand the actual mission, I also look for its trajectory, the frame the picture uses and the way the team maintains that path.
