Step off an exposed pavement and into the shade of a tree on a summer day. In a few metres the amount of solar radiation reaching your body and the ground around you can change. That shade is a function a street or square can be designed to provide, just like a route needs space for people to walk.

Water also matters to the design. [Rain gardens](/blog/giardini-pioggia-deflusso-tetti) address runoff, while the guide to [water footprints](/blog/impronta-idrica-contesto) shows why local water conditions affect how to interpret a quantity used for irrigation.

## Separate the ways a tree changes the microclimate

A canopy intercepts some sunlight before it reaches people and surfaces. Evapotranspiration also affects energy and water exchange. Their effects depend on the species, canopy size, soil water and local conditions. Surface temperature, air temperature and radiant heat exposure describe different aspects of what someone feels. A measurement at a single point can miss the benefit of shade along a walking route.

## Design for a canopy that will last

A planted tree needs enough soil volume, space for roots and a water and maintenance plan. Count the trees that will provide useful shade after growth as well as the ones installed today. Think about their distribution. A shaded path used daily can meet a particular need even if the same amount of greenery in an outlying space would have little effect on that trip.

## Measure when people use the space

Compare shaded and exposed points at relevant times, recording the conditions and what the measurements represent. Include who can reach the shaded places and when they need them. The European Environment Agency considers urban vegetation and nature-based solutions alongside building measures for cooling. For a local project, I would define the intended shade hours and users first, then check that the tree and its maintenance can deliver them.
