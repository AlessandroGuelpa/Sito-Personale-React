A lunar mission may be easiest to follow during launch, with the rocket climbing under a trail of light. The return raises another engineering problem. Orion has kinetic energy to lose as it enters the atmosphere, and the thermal protection system has to keep the structure within its limits during that process.

[Orion's radiators](/blog/orion-controllo-termico-radiatori-vuoto) manage heat during other phases of flight. The heat shield is designed for the concentrated heat load of atmospheric entry.

## Speed changes the energy budget

Kinetic energy is **E = ½mv²**. For the same mass, doubling the speed gives four times the kinetic energy. That relation describes the energy of the motion. The part that reaches the vehicle as heat depends on how the capsule interacts with the atmosphere. A return from a lunar trajectory faces higher entry speeds than a typical return from low Earth orbit. Demonstrating the heat shield was a central objective of Artemis I.

## Use the material and the trajectory together

Air compresses and heats ahead of the capsule. The shield limits how much heat reaches the structure. Orion's ablative material undergoes a controlled transformation, and part of it is consumed as it protects the vehicle. The trajectory affects when the strongest heating and deceleration occur and how long they last.

## Consider the whole entry profile

I look at the initial energy, the shape of the capsule and its path through the atmosphere together. A statement of peak temperature gives only part of the load. How long the protection is exposed matters too. Ground tests and flight evidence can then be interpreted against the conditions the vehicle actually has to handle. The material, flight controls and entry profile all contribute to the same outcome.
