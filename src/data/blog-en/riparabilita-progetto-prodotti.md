A device can work for years and become unusable when an inexpensive component fails. Product design helps determine how serious that failure becomes. Access to the worn part, the available replacement and the support around it all shape whether the device can return to service.

When repair is no longer practical, [electronic waste collection](/blog/raee-elettronica-fine-vita) provides another route. The [quality of collected material](/blog/riciclo-raccolta-qualita-materiale) affects what that route can recover.

## Follow a small failure through the design

Imagine two devices with the same fault in their power connector. One has a connector you can reach and replace. In the other device the connector is embedded in an expensive assembly that is hard to open without damaging other parts. The underlying fault can be identical while the time, cost and likelihood of repair differ. A part that is easy to remove still needs to be obtainable, and the repairer needs a way to install and test it.

## Look at the rest of the repair process

The European Commission's Joint Research Centre links repairability to design and support choices. I would trace how someone reaches parts likely to wear out, which tools they need and how the product is closed again. Instructions and spare parts affect whether that path is usable in practice.

Also check software support. A physically repairable device can lose essential functions if it can no longer get the updates or compatible software it needs. Physical life and useful service life belong in the same comparison.

## Ask what a predictable failure would cost

Whether repair makes sense depends on the defect, remaining service life, efficiency and impact of replacing the device. Before buying, I would ask what happens when its most likely wear part fails. If replacement involves discarding a larger working assembly, include that possibility when considering the price and useful life of the product.
