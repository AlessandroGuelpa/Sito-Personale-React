“Waste less food” is easy to agree with. It leaves open where to change a routine. Buying more than the household needs, making portions that regularly leave leftovers and forgetting food already in the fridge are different problems. You can look for which one happens before changing the shopping list.

A [water footprint](/blog/impronta-idrica-contesto) can include water used to produce discarded food. [Life cycle assessment](/blog/lca-confini-unita-funzionale) shows why a comparison also needs a clearly defined scope.

## Give the waste a useful description

The UNEP Food Waste Index distinguishes households, food service and retail and pays attention to how each is measured. A household can test the same idea with a short log. Record the food, quantity, reason for discarding it and whether it was edible. A pot of leftovers and unavoidable peels can weigh the same while suggesting different changes to the routine.

## Try the intervention that fits the pattern

If you often find forgotten packages, put the items you need to use next somewhere visible and check the shelves before shopping. If most edible waste comes from portions left on plates, try adjusting how much you prepare. These are changes to test. Record a second comparable period and account for how many people were present and how many meals they ate at home.

## Compare the reason as well as the weight

Two weeks with the same total can reflect more home cooking in one case and more unopened products in the other. Use the reason and the amount together. A short period of measurement is enough if it identifies a repeatable change and gives you a way to see whether it helped. A routine that makes the right items easier to find or the usual portions easier to prepare can then carry on without a permanent log.
