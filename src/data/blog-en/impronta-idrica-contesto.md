A product can require water long before it reaches the person who buys it. Growing raw materials and processing them may use water at different points in the supply chain. Virtual water helps make that hidden use visible. To interpret a total in litres, also ask what kind of water it represents, where the effects take place and when they occur.

[Measuring food waste](/blog/spreco-alimentare-misurare-prima) gives another case where the measurement needs clear boundaries. [Rain gardens](/blog/giardini-pioggia-deflusso-tetti) show how rainfall can matter at a local scale.

## Read the components separately

Water footprint methods commonly distinguish green, blue and grey water. Green water refers to rainwater used in production. Blue water represents consumption of surface water and groundwater. The grey water footprint is an indicator of the theoretical volume needed to assimilate a pollutant load under defined water quality criteria. The last figure describes the conditions of that model. It should be read in terms of that definition when comparing it with figures for physical water use.

## Put the volume in place and time

Using the same volume can have different consequences in a basin with abundant supply and in a basin in a scarce season. A single annual total can hide this difference. Check the unit, which parts of the supply chain are included and where the inputs come from before comparing two products.

## Ask for the data behind the comparison

Suppose two suppliers offer the same material. I would ask for the component breakdown, the relevant basins and the period represented by the data. Then I would compare those figures with the conditions under which water was used. A footprint becomes more informative when a reader can see those inputs and judge what a difference in litres represents.
