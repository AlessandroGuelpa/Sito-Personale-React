A launch vehicle can be ready on the pad and still have to wait for a specific time. Earth rotates, the Moon moves, and the planned flight has to satisfy several conditions together. The countdown coordinates the operations. The launch window describes when the trajectory and mission conditions can work.

The transfer will meet the Moon after departure. [Powered lunar landing](/blog/allunaggio-paracadute-propulsione) deals with a later part of the flight, and [Lagrange points](/blog/punti-lagrange-equilibrio-instabile) demonstrate other orbital constraints.

## Account for where the Moon will be

A transfer's geometry must fit where the vehicle and its destination will be as the flight unfolds. The Canadian Space Agency uses Artemis II to illustrate the required alignment of Earth, the Moon and Orion, including how manoeuvres are arranged. The example shows why the departure time affects more than when the pad is clear.

## Fit the conditions of the mission

The orbital geometry, launch vehicle performance, lighting and conditions at return can all contribute to the set of viable departure times. The decisive combination is specific to the mission. Even on a day with a usable opportunity, only a limited interval may meet those conditions. A problem on the pad can cause the whole interval to pass before the vehicle is ready again.

## Reassess the opportunity after a delay

The system keeps moving while the launch team waits. A later opportunity can require different flight parameters or operations. The schedule from the previous attempt may need to change even after the original technical problem has been addressed.

When I hear a launch broadcast announce a delay, I look for what the current window was designed to satisfy. Hardware readiness is one condition the mission needs. A departure time also has to match the geometry and the flight plan that follow it.
