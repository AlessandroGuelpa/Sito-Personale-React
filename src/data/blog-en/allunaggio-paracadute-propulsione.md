A parachute slows a descent by interacting with the surrounding air. Its large surface area creates drag as the vehicle moves through that fluid. A lunar lander needs a different way to slow down because the Moon’s exosphere is extremely thin.

[Mass, weight and inertia on the Moon](/blog/luna-massa-peso-inerzia) help explain why a vehicle still needs to change its velocity in low gravity. [Lunar launch windows and orbits](/blog/finestre-lancio-luna-orbite) describe some of the trajectory constraints that come before the descent.

## Provide the braking force with propulsion

The lunar exosphere is too tenuous for an ordinary parachute or wing to supply the braking force needed for a landing. ESA describes propulsion as the means of slowing the lunar descent. The vehicle uses thrust to change its motion before reaching the surface.

## Start with the velocity the lander already has

A vehicle in orbit is moving across the Moon’s surface as well as changing its altitude during descent. A controlled landing therefore needs to manage the full velocity of the vehicle. The approach trajectory affects propellant use, the time available and how the sensors see the landing area. Touchdown comes after a sequence of controlled changes to position and velocity.

## Test the guidance in the intended environment

For a conceptual design, I would start with questions about navigation, thrust control and how the vehicle detects obstacles. Then I would ask how it responds when a measurement is late or the terrain differs from the expected model. The detailed solution depends on the mission profile, vehicle mass, sensors and engines.

Before carrying an approach over from Earth, check whether the physical mechanism it uses will work in the destination environment. For a parachute, the availability of enough surrounding fluid determines whether it can provide useful drag.
