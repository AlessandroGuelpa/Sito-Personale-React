When packaging goes into a recycling bin, it enters a chain that has to sort it, treat it and find a use for the recovered material. Collection gives that chain a chance to work. Each of the later steps affects the quantity and quality that can return to use.

[Electronic waste](/blog/raee-elettronica-fine-vita) illustrates a stream with very different materials in the same object. [Repairable products](/blog/riparabilita-progetto-prodotti) can also stay in service longer before entering any waste treatment process.

## Start with the quality of the incoming stream

Sorting facilities receive mixed objects. Foreign items, contamination and materials that are hard to separate can slow sorting or reduce the quality of the output. I recognise the same question in data integrations. A file made it across the connection, and the receiving system still needs to check whether the fields meet its contract. In both cases the inputs determine what the next step can work with.

## Follow local collection instructions

A material that can be recycled in one process may appear in objects a local collection service cannot accept. Shape, composition and the available treatment facilities matter. Check the current instructions from the local operator for the actual object. A symbol or a general description of the material gives only part of the information.

## Count what the process recovers

When comparing systems, look at the amount collected, what sorting rejects and how much usable material leaves treatment. Also ask what the recovered material can replace in a new product. Those questions give more detail than collection tonnage alone.

Waste prevention, reuse, longer product life and recycling operate at different points. Putting a package in the right bin is a concrete part of the process for the consumer. Judging the environmental outcome means following it after collection and looking at the materials that return to use.
