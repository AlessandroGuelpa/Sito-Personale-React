A power system needs enough electricity where and when people use it. Annual energy generation cannot tell you whether the system can meet a demand peak on a particular evening or move electricity through a congested part of the network. Availability over time and transmission capacity are part of the same problem.

The distinction between [power in kW and energy in kWh](/blog/kw-kwh-potenza-energia) helps here. The [carbon intensity of electricity](/blog/elettricita-intensita-carbonica) can also change with the generation mix during the day.

## What is demand flexibility?

It is the ability to change when or how much electricity is drawn while meeting service constraints. A pump can fill a tank earlier if there is spare capacity. Charging can start later if it still delivers the required energy by departure time. Estimating the available flexibility requires the movable kW, duration, scheduling window and maximum recovery power.

## Put generation and demand on a timeline

Two plants with the same annual production may generate at very different hours. Two consumers with the same annual total may put different demands on the network if one concentrates use in a short peak. The IEA's Electricity 2026 report describes several forms of flexibility that can help balance evolving supply and demand. Which ones are useful depends on when and where the mismatch occurs.

## Read the two battery ratings together

Suppose a battery can deliver 1 MW and store 4 MWh of usable energy in a simplified calculation. At its rated power, it could deliver power for about four hours before accounting for losses and operational limits. Doubling the power rating with the same available energy changes how quickly it can discharge. The energy required to cover a longer mismatch is a separate design question.

## Identify demand that can move

Some activities can shift or reduce their load for a period while still meeting the service requirement. A short surge, local congestion and low generation over several consecutive hours each place different demands on storage, networks, generation and flexible consumption. Before comparing proposals, ask for the load and supply profiles, where the bottleneck is and how long it lasts. Those inputs make it possible to tell which flexibility measure can actually help.

## Calculate the shift from an hourly load profile

A fleet needs 24 kWh at the meter by 7 a.m. At a steady 6 kW it takes four hours. Moving charging from 6–10 p.m. to 2–6 a.m. shifts all 24 kWh and reduces fleet demand in the original time slot by 6 kW. The new time slot gains the same 6 kW. If many fleets start at 2 a.m., the new peak can become the bottleneck. Check the total site load and stagger starts when needed.

Compare the measured profile with a baseline from comparable days. Check the peak in the requested interval and again over the following hours, then verify that the batteries have received the required energy by 7 a.m. Assess the network benefit, tariff cost and [electricity carbon intensity](/blog/elettricita-intensita-carbonica) separately, since each measure depends on different conditions.
