During a downpour, a gutter sends the rain that hits a roof into a downpipe. If the downpipe opens onto a paved yard, the water continues along the surface. When many roofs and paved areas drain quickly into the same network, the flow rate can rise sharply. A rain garden intercepts water along that route.

A rain garden manages runoff. [Urban trees](/blog/alberi-citta-calore-progetto) can contribute shade and change the local microclimate. Combining those functions takes space, suitable soil and maintenance. When assessing water consumption more broadly, consider [the context of a water footprint](/blog/impronta-idrica-contesto).

## A planted depression that receives runoff

A rain garden is a shallow planted depression into which runoff is directed. It temporarily holds water and gives it time to infiltrate. How much reaches the ground depends on the soil and how wet it already is. Some designs also include a drain beneath the filter layer.

The route into the garden matters. If the yard slopes towards the street, a planted bed beside that path can stay almost dry while the street drain receives the runoff.

## The same 1,200 litres in two storms

Imagine a roof with an area of 80 m² when viewed from above. One millimetre of rain over one square metre is one litre. A 15 mm rainfall on that roof therefore gives 1,200 litres.

If all that water reaches the downpipe in the time it takes to fall, an hour of rain gives an average of 20 litres per minute. If it falls within ten minutes, the average is 120 litres per minute. The volume is the same and the second storm can fill the depression much faster. These are simplified average rates. Sizing also requires information on local rainfall and the soil’s infiltration capacity.

## Follow the water through to the overflow

I would start by mapping the yard. Mark where the downpipe discharges, the direction of flow and where excess water can go when the garden fills. A design needs an overflow leading to a suitable outlet away from entrances and foundations. After rain, check that leaves have not blocked the inlet and record how long the depression takes to empty. If runoff bypasses the garden, check the inlet and slope before enlarging it.
